<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a project you are proud of" is a deep-dive prompt, not
a show-and-tell. The interviewer will drill into it for ten minutes
or more, so the project you pick must survive drilling.

Interviewers score three things. Was the problem real, with a
constraint that forced choices. Were the decisions yours, with
trade-offs you can explain. And can you criticise your own work now,
with hindsight.

The trap is picking the biggest project. Size does not survive
drilling; decisions do. Pick the project where you made the hardest
calls, even if it was smaller, because the follow-ups will be about
those calls.

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong project story sounds like. This is an
illustrative example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using
the template below.

**Situation.** "The project I am proudest of is the checkout rebuild
I led last year. Completion had stalled at 61 percent, support kept
getting payment error complaints, and we could not change the billing
system, only the frontend."

**Task.** "My job was to lift completion without touching billing,
which meant every improvement had to come from the form, the flow,
or the words on screen."

**Action.** "I made three decisions. First, validation errors show
next to the field that caused them instead of at the top of the
form, because support logs showed users could not find the error.
Second, a review step before payment, because users told support
they were afraid of paying twice. Third, I rewrote the three most
complained-about messages with support, instead of guessing better
words myself. The trade-off I accepted was scope: I cut the
comparison table and the animations to protect the calculator and
the checkout link."

**Result.** "Completion rose from 61 percent to 74 percent over two
months, and payment error tickets fell by about a third. What I
would change now is testing on the oldest browser we support before
opening the pull request, because our one incident came from a method
that browser did not support."

The answer works because the constraint forces the decisions, each
decision has a reason tied to evidence, and the ending criticises the
work honestly. That ending is what senior panels listen for.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### Project deep-dive template

Prepare these five blocks for one project before the interview.
Panels drill in this order: problem, decisions, trade-offs, problems,
hindsight.

- **Problem and constraint.** "The problem was [user or business
  pain with a number]. The constraint was [what you could not
  change]." Constraints force decisions. A project with no constraint
  has no story.
- **Your role.** "I [owned / led / built] [surface], and the team
  was [size]." Say what was yours and what was shared, precisely.
- **Three decisions.** For each: "I chose [X] over [Y] because
  [evidence]." Three is the right count. One sounds thin, five
  sounds rehearsed.
- **What broke.** "The hardest problem was [incident or setback],
  and I handled it by [rollback / fix / change]." A project with no
  problems sounds invented. Bring one real one.
- **What you would change.** "With what I know now I would [one
  specific change]." This is the senior signal. Never answer "nothing."

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I built a checkout app with React and TypeScript and
Node. It had a nice UI and used Redux and it worked really well and
users liked it. I am proud of it because it was a big project."

Why it fails: no problem, no constraint, no decisions, no trade-offs.
It is a stack list. A panel cannot drill into a stack list, so they
will move on, unimpressed.

Strong answer: "The project I am proudest of is the checkout rebuild
I led last year. The problem was that completion had stalled at 61
percent and support kept getting payment error complaints. The
constraint was that we could not change the billing system, only the
frontend. I chose to rebuild validation so errors show next to the
field that caused them, added a review step before payment, and
rewrote the three most complained-about messages with support."

Why it works: problem with a number, constraint stated, three
decisions each tied to evidence, and the trade-off (scope cut) is
visible in the full answer. Every sentence invites a good follow-up.

### Drill-down questions to prepare

Panels drill into projects with the same questions. Prepare one-line
answers for each:

- "Why did you choose that technology?" Evidence, not preference.
  The alternative you rejected, and why.
- "What was the hardest problem?" One real incident, with the fix
  and the systemic change afterwards.
- "How did you measure success?" The before and after number, and
  where it came from (analytics, support, users).
- "What did you personally do versus the team?" Precise split. Panels
  check this against the rest of your story.
- "What would you do differently now?" One specific change, never
  "nothing." Hindsight is the senior signal.
- "How would you scale it to ten times the traffic?" One bottleneck
  you would hit first, and what you would change. Even for frontend
  projects, have an answer.

### Traps

- Picking the biggest project instead of the one with the hardest
  decisions. Decisions survive drilling; size does not.
- A story with no problems. If nothing broke, the panel assumes you
  are hiding it or were not close to the work.
- Answering "nothing" to what you would change. It signals no growth
  since the project.
- Claiming team work as solo work. Panels cross-check with other
  answers. Keep the split precise.

### Follow-up prep

- "Why did you choose that technology?" Your decision block, with
  the rejected alternative named.
- "What was the hardest problem in that project?" Your what-broke
  block, told with the fix first order if users were affected.
- "What would you do differently now?" Your hindsight block, one
  specific change, stated without apology.
