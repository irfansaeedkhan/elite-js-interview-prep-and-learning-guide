<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague" tests whether
you can fight for a good idea without damaging the relationship.
Every senior hire gets asked some version of this, because senior
work is mostly disagreements handled well.

Interviewers score three things. Did you disagree about the work,
with reasons, rather than about the person. Did you bring evidence
instead of louder opinions. And did you commit to the decision once
it was made, even if you lost.

The trap is the story where you win by being right. "I proved them
wrong and we did it my way" tells the panel you will be hard to work
with. The best version often ends with a compromise, or with you
losing gracefully.

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "A senior engineer proposed rebuilding our table
component from scratch. It had real problems, slow sorting and broken
keyboard navigation, and a rebuild felt like the clean answer."

**Task.** "I thought the fix was smaller than a rebuild, but I was
the less senior person, and arguing the point in a review would have
made it about status instead of the component."

**Action.** "I took the complaint list from support and sorted it by
frequency. Sorting and keyboard issues covered most reports, so I
built a small prototype that fixed exactly those two things and
measured it against the same cases. Then I showed the prototype to
him privately first, with the numbers, and said the rebuild was still
the right call if the prototype failed."

**Result.** "We agreed to fix first and revisit a rebuild next
quarter. The fix shipped in a week, support tickets on the table fell
by half, and the rebuild never became necessary. He later asked me to
prototype first on another disputed change, which is the part I am
most pleased about."

The answer works because the disagreement is handled with evidence
and privacy, not a public win. The relationship improves, which is
the real result.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "A colleague proposed [X] and I believed [Y]."
  State both positions fairly. If you cannot state their side well,
  you did not understand the disagreement.
- **Task.** "I needed to [resolve it] without [damaging the
  relationship / making it about status]." Name the constraint.
- **Action.** "I [brought evidence: a prototype, data, a test, a
  user report]." Evidence is the only move that scales. Also note
  where you raised it: privately first beats publicly.
- **Result.** "We decided [outcome], and [what happened to the
  relationship]." A compromise or a graceful loss both count. A
  humiliation does not.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "A colleague wanted to rebuild a component and I knew
that was wrong, so I explained why in the team meeting and everyone
agreed with me. We fixed it instead and it worked out."

Why it fails: the other person is a straw man, the win is public,
and there is no evidence, only persuasion. Panels hear "difficult to
work with" in this story.

Strong answer: "A senior engineer wanted to rebuild our table
component from scratch, and I thought we should fix the existing
one. Instead of arguing in the review, I built a small prototype of
the fix and measured it against the complaints we actually had. The
prototype fixed the sorting bug and the keyboard issue, which covered
most reports, so we agreed to fix first and revisit a rebuild in the
next quarter."

Why it works: both positions are stated fairly, the evidence is a
prototype with measurements, the discussion happens privately, and
the outcome is a compromise with a review date, not a victory lap.

### Traps

- Winning publicly. If your story ends with someone embarrassed, it
  is a bad story even if you were right.
- Disagreeing with nobody. "We all agreed in the end" dodges the
  question. Name a real disagreement.
- No evidence. "I explained my reasoning" is not evidence. A
  prototype, a number, or a user report is.
- Refusing to commit. If you lost and then kept fighting the
  decision, say so honestly and say what you learned, or pick another
  story.

### Follow-up prep

- "What do you do when you lose an argument at work?" Have a real
  loss ready. "I commit and I note what would change my mind" is a
  strong line.
- "How do you disagree with someone senior to you?" Private first,
  evidence second, status never. That is the whole answer.
- "Have you ever changed your mind in a disagreement?" Say yes and
  name the evidence that changed it. Changing your mind on evidence
  is a strength, not a weakness.
