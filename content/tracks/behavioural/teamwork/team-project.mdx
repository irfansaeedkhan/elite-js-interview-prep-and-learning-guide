<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you worked effectively as part of a team" tests
whether you can be a good colleague, not just a good coder.
Interviewers listen for your contribution inside a group result.

They score three things. Did you do something specific that only you
did. Did you make the team faster or the work better, not just your
own ticket. And can you describe the result without claiming the
whole team's credit.

The trap is "we." A story told entirely in "we built, we shipped, we
succeeded" hides your part. Interviewers cannot score what they
cannot see. Use "I" for your actions and "we" for the shared result.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Last year our team of five rebuilt the onboarding
flow. I owned the frontend form and the progress states, and design
and backend were building against each other without a shared
contract."

**Task.** "We had six weeks, and the first version of the flow had
already slipped once because the form steps changed after backend
built to the old ones."

**Action.** "I wrote a one-page contract for the form steps, the
fields on each step, and the error cases, then walked design and
backend through it in one meeting. When anyone wanted to change a
step after that, we changed the contract first. I also set up a short
daily check-in for the last two weeks so mismatches surfaced the same
day instead of at the end of the sprint."

**Result.** "We shipped in six weeks with no rework round, and
activation in the first week rose from 38 percent to 51 percent. The
contract became the template the team used for the next flow rebuild."

The answer works because the "I" actions are visible (the contract,
the meeting, the check-in) and the "we" result is shared honestly.
One document did the teamwork, not a claim about being a team player.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "Our team was building [project] and [the
  coordination problem]." Name the problem, not just the project.
- **Task.** "My part was [your surface or responsibility], and the
  team needed [the shared outcome]."
- **Action.** "I [the specific thing you did that helped the team,
  not just your ticket]." A document, a meeting, a decision, a
  handover. Something others used.
- **Result.** "We [shared result with a number], and [what lasted
  after the project]." Lasting effects beat one-off wins.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "We worked really well together on the onboarding
rebuild. Everyone communicated well and we all helped each other, and
we shipped on time and users liked it."

Why it fails: there is no "I" in it at all. No action, no moment, no
number. It claims teamwork without showing any.

Strong answer: "Last year our team rebuilt the onboarding flow, and
my part was the frontend form and the progress states. Early on I
noticed design and backend were making assumptions about each other,
so I wrote a one-page contract for the form steps, the fields, and
the error cases, and got both sides to agree on it before we built.
That cut two rounds of rework."

Why it works: the contribution is one concrete artefact, the benefit
is stated (two rounds of rework cut), and the team result is shared
without hiding the speaker's part.

### Traps

- The all-"we" story. If the interviewer cannot find you in the
  answer, rewrite it.
- Claiming the team's result as yours. "I shipped the flow" when five
  people built it reads as dishonest, not confident.
- A story with no friction. A team story where nothing went wrong
  sounds invented. Name the coordination problem.
- Summarising a whole project. One moment, told fully, beats a tour
  of six months.

### Follow-up prep

- "What was your specific contribution?" Your answer should already
  contain it. If the panel has to ask, your first answer hid it.
- "How did you handle disagreements in that team?" Have one real
  disagreement ready. The disagreement topic in this track is good
  preparation.
- "What would you do differently on that project?" Pick one real
  change, not a humble-brag. "I would have written the contract a
  week earlier" is honest and small.
