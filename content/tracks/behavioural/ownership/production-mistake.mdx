<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time something you shipped went wrong" is the
question senior panels trust most, because everyone has a story and
the bad ones are easy to spot. It tests ownership under embarrassment.

Interviewers score four things, in order. Did you own it quickly.
Did you fix it before explaining it. Did you find the real cause.
And did you change the system so it cannot happen the same way again.

The trap is the story with no owner. "The code had a bug" or "QA
missed it" or "the browser was old" all move blame somewhere else.
Panels hear blame-shifting instantly, and it is the fastest way to
fail a behavioural round.

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "I shipped a form change on a Friday morning, and by
midday support was reporting that checkout failed for users on an
older browser. Our monitoring had not caught it because the error
looked like user abandonment, not a crash."

**Task.** "I owned the change, so I owned the incident. The order
was fixed first, explain second."

**Action.** "I posted in the team channel within the hour that the
change was mine and I was rolling it back. The rollback took twenty
minutes. Then I reproduced the bug on the old browser and found a
method it did not support. The fix was a small polyfill plus a test
that runs on that browser. I also added a browser smoke check to the
release checklist, and I wrote the incident note myself."

**Result.** "Checkout worked again the same day, and the smoke check
has caught two similar issues before shipping since. The team did
not treat the incident as a black mark, because the rollback was
fast and the systemic fix was mine too. What I check now that I did
not check then is simple: I run the change on the oldest browser we
support before I open the pull request."

The answer works because the order is right (own, roll back, diagnose,
systemic fix) and the blame goes nowhere. The last line is the
cheapest proof of learning in any behavioural answer: a habit that
changed.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "I shipped [change], and [what broke, found how]."
  Say how it was found. "Support told us" is honest and common.
- **Task.** "I owned it, so the order was [fix first, explain
  second]." Say the order explicitly. Panels score it.
- **Action.** "I [owned it publicly, rolled back, reproduced,
  fixed, tested]." Then the systemic part: "I added [checklist,
  test, monitor] so the class of bug is caught next time."
- **Result.** "[Restored behaviour and time], and [what you check
  now that you did not check then]." The changed habit is the real
  result.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "There was a bug in a form I worked on that broke
checkout for some users. QA should have caught it, and the old browser
was the problem really. We fixed it eventually and it was fine."

Why it fails: no owner, no timeline, no rollback, no systemic fix.
Every sentence moves blame somewhere else, and "eventually" hides
how long users were broken.

Strong answer: "I shipped a form change that broke checkout for
users on an older browser, and we found out from support, not from
our monitoring. I owned it in the team channel within the hour,
rolled the change back first, and then found the cause, which was a
method the old browser did not support. The fix was a small polyfill
plus a test on that browser, and I added a browser smoke check to the
release checklist."

Why it works: the speaker owns it in the first sentence, the rollback
comes before the diagnosis, and the fix has two layers (the bug and
the system that missed it). No blame anywhere.

### Traps

- Blame in any direction. Not QA, not the reviewer, not the tool,
  not the user. If blame appears, the story fails.
- Fix without rollback. Debugging while users are broken is the
  wrong order. Roll back first.
- Apology without systemic fix. "I learned to be careful" is not a
  fix. A test, a checklist, or a monitor is.
- A trivial mistake. "I had a typo once" dodges the question. Pick
  a real incident with real users affected.

### Follow-up prep

- "How did the team react when you owned it?" Honest answer: fast
  ownership usually earns trust, and the systemic fix is what people
  remember.
- "What do you do differently before shipping now?" Name the habit
  from your result beat. Keep it identical to your story.
- "Tell me about a time you caught someone else's mistake." Keep it
  kind and private. The helped-teammate topic is good preparation.
