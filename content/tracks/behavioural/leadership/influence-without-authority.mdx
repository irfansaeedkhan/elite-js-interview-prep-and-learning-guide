<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you influenced a decision without having
authority" is the senior leadership question for people without a
title. It tests whether you can move a decision you do not own.

Interviewers score three things. Did you understand what the
decision maker actually cared about. Did you bring evidence that
spoke to that concern, not just your own. And did the decision move,
even partly.

The trap is the campaign story. Lobbying everyone, escalating to a
manager, or winning by persistence all signal that you cannot
influence cleanly. The best version is quiet: data, one private
conversation, a decision.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Our team kept deferring accessibility fixes because
they never won against feature work in planning. I cared about it,
but I had no authority to change priorities, and arguing about values
in planning had already failed twice."

**Task.** "I needed the product manager to choose three fixes
voluntarily, which meant I had to speak to her concern, which was
sprint capacity, not my concern, which was principle."

**Action.** "I collected the data first. I counted support tickets
for a month that mentioned keyboard or screen reader problems, and I
estimated the top three fixes at about two days total. I took that to
her privately before planning, framed as a trade, not a demand: two
days for a measurable drop in a ticket class. I also named which
feature work I was not asking to delay, so the ask was bounded."

**Result.** "She put the three fixes in the next sprint. They
shipped, and the related tickets fell by half the next month. The
next time I raised an accessibility item, it got discussed on its
numbers instead of deferred by default, which was the real win."

The answer works because the influence has a mechanism (data plus a
bounded private ask) and the result includes a lasting change in how
the topic gets discussed. That is influence, not a single win.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "[Decision] was owned by [someone else], and the
  default outcome was [what you wanted to change]." Name the owner
  and the default.
- **Task.** "I needed [decision maker] to choose [change], so I had
  to speak to [their concern], not mine." Finding their concern is
  the work. Ask, or infer from what they protect.
- **Action.** "I brought [evidence in their terms], privately, with
  a bounded ask." Evidence plus privacy plus bounds. A public
  campaign is the opposite of influence.
- **Result.** "[Decision moved, even partly], and [how the topic is
  handled now]." A partial win that changes the process beats a
  total win that changes nothing else.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I really believed in accessibility, so I kept raising
it in planning and talked to a lot of people about it until the team
finally agreed to fix some issues. It took persistence but we got
there."

Why it fails: no evidence, no decision maker, no bounded ask. It
describes wearing people down, which panels read as politics, not
influence.

Strong answer: "Our team kept deferring accessibility fixes because
they never won against feature work in planning. I had no authority
to change priorities, so I collected the data first. I counted how
many support tickets in a month mentioned keyboard or screen reader
problems, and I showed that fixing the top three issues would take
about two days. I took that to the product manager privately, and she
agreed to put the three fixes in the next sprint."

Why it works: the concern is named (sprint capacity), the evidence
is in those terms (tickets and days), the conversation is private,
and the ask is bounded (three fixes, two days). That is a mechanism,
not persistence.

### Traps

- Escalation as influence. Going over someone's head is not
  influence. It is the failure mode of influence.
- Arguing values to a capacity audience. If the decision maker cares
  about time, bring time numbers, not principles.
- The unbounded ask. "Fix accessibility" is not an ask. "Three fixes,
  two days" is.
- Claiming a win you did not cause. If the decision was already
  moving, say so, and describe your part honestly.

### Follow-up prep

- "How do you handle it when stakeholders ignore data?" Have an
  answer: ask what evidence would change their mind, or accept the
  decision and note the review point.
- "Have you ever failed to influence a decision?" Say yes, briefly,
  with what you misread about the decision maker's concern.
- "How do you build allies for an idea?" One private conversation
  at a time, starting with the person most affected by the problem.
