<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you mentored or coached someone" tests whether
you can grow another person on purpose. Helping once is kindness.
Mentoring is a system with a goal, a cadence, and an end state.

Interviewers score three things. Did you set a goal with the person,
not for them. Did you give them work that stretched them, with real
review. And did they end up independent, not dependent on you.

The trap is the effort story. "I spent hours helping him every week"
measures your time, not their growth. If the person still needs you
at the end of the story, the mentoring failed, however kind it was.

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "A new graduate joined our team and spent a month on
small bug fixes, but he still needed help starting every ticket. He
was not failing, he was just never given a path to independence."

**Task.** "I asked him what he wanted to be able to do alone in three
months. He said build a small feature end to end, including tests.
That became our goal, his words, not mine."

**Action.** "We broke the goal into steps, starting with a settings
page that touched the frontend, the API call, and the tests. I
reviewed his work twice a week, and I wrote the notes down instead of
giving quick verbal fixes, so he could reread them. When he got
stuck, I asked what he had ruled out before I suggested anything,
the same method from the helped-teammate story, applied as a system."

**Result.** "In three months he shipped the settings page alone,
including the tests. He now reviews bug fixes for the next new
joiner, which is the real result: the mentoring reproduced itself."

The answer works because the goal is the mentee's, the cadence is
stated (twice a week, written notes), and the end state is
independence plus paying it forward. That is a system, not a favour.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "[Person] was at [starting point], and [the gap to
  independence]." Name the gap concretely, not "he was junior."
- **Task.** "We agreed a goal: [their words for what they wanted to
  do alone, with a timeframe]." Their words matter. A goal imposed
  by you is an assignment, not mentoring.
- **Action.** "We broke it into [steps], with [cadence and review
  method]." Cadence (twice a week) and method (written notes) are
  what make it a system.
- **Result.** "[They shipped something alone], and [they now help
  someone else / no longer need you for this]." Independence is the
  only result that counts.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I mentored a new graduate by helping him whenever he
got stuck and answering his questions. I spent a lot of time with him
and he really appreciated it and got much better."

Why it fails: no goal, no cadence, no end state. It describes
availability, not mentoring, and "got much better" is not a result.

Strong answer: "A new graduate joined our team and was given small
bug fixes, but after a month he still needed help starting every
ticket. I asked him what he wanted to be able to do alone in three
months, and he said build a small feature end to end. We broke that
into steps, and I reviewed his work twice a week with written notes
instead of quick verbal fixes. In three months he shipped a settings
page alone, including the tests."

Why it works: the goal is theirs, the steps and cadence are stated,
the review method is concrete, and the result is independent delivery
with tests. Every sentence earns its place.

### Traps

- Measuring your effort. Hours spent are not a result. Their
  independence is.
- The imposed goal. If you chose the goal, say so honestly, or pick
  a story where the goal was shared.
- No end state. If the story ends with them still needing you, it
  is a dependency story, not a mentoring story.
- Mentoring as rescuing. Same trap as the helped-teammate topic:
  doing their work for them is not mentoring.

### Follow-up prep

- "How do you adapt mentoring to different people?" Have one
  contrast ready: one person needed structure, another needed room,
  and you adjusted the cadence, not the goal.
- "What do you do when a mentee is not improving?" Name the honest
  conversation first, then the changed plan, then escalation if
  needed. Never pretend everyone improves.
- "Who mentored you, and what did you take from it?" Name a person
  and a specific method you still use. It shows the chain is real.
