<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you improved a process or codebase for your
team" tests whether you leave things better than you found them.
Interviewers want an improvement the team actually adopted, not a
refactor you enjoyed.

They score three things. Did the improvement start from a cost the
team felt, not a taste preference. Did others adopt it without being
forced. And did it survive after you stopped pushing it.

The trap is the personal refactor. "I cleaned up the codebase" with
no adoption, no measurement, and no one else using it is a hobby,
not an improvement. Adoption is the result.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Our team had no shared pattern for data fetching. Every
screen handled loading and error states differently, reviews kept
relitigating the same choices, and new joiners copied whichever
screen they opened first."

**Task.** "I wanted one default pattern that made the right thing
the easy thing, not a rule people had to remember."

**Action.** "I built one small hook with loading, error, and retry
states, and I converted two example screens to it so the team could
see it working instead of reading a proposal. Then I added it to our
review checklist as the default, which meant reviewers suggested it
without me being in the room. I deliberately kept it small: three
states, no extra features, so adopting it cost less than arguing
about it."

**Result.** "Within a month four screens used it, review comments
about fetching dropped sharply, and a new joiner shipped a data
screen in his first week by copying the pattern. The pattern is
still the default, and I have not touched it in months, which is how
I know it stuck."

The answer works because adoption is the result, the mechanism is
"make it the easy path," and survival without the author is stated
explicitly. That last line is the proof most improvement stories
forget.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "[Cost the team felt]: [repeated argument / slow
  onboarding / recurring bug class]." The cost must be shared, not
  personal annoyance.
- **Task.** "I wanted [a default that makes the right thing easy],
  not a rule." Defaults beat rules because they survive without
  enforcement.
- **Action.** "I built [small thing], showed it on [example
  screens], and made it the default via [checklist / template /
  starter]." Example screens beat proposals. The checklist is how
  adoption happens without you.
- **Result.** "[Adoption number], [before and after with the same
  measure], and [it survives without you]." Survival is the final
  proof.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I noticed our codebase was messy, so I spent some time
cleaning it up and refactoring the data fetching code. It was much
cleaner afterwards and I felt good about it."

Why it fails: no shared cost, no adoption, no measurement, no one
else involved. It is a personal project, not a team improvement.

Strong answer: "Our team had no shared pattern for data fetching, so
every screen handled loading and error states differently and reviews
kept relitigating the same choices. I proposed one small hook with
loading, error, and retry states, wrote it with two example screens,
and added it to our review checklist as the default. Within a month
four screens used it."

Why it works: the cost is shared (relitigated reviews), the artefact
is small and shown working, adoption is counted (four screens), and
the checklist makes adoption happen without the author.

### Traps

- Taste as cost. "It was messy" is not a cost. Repeated review
  arguments, slow onboarding, and recurring bugs are.
- No adoption number. If nobody used it, it did not improve anything.
- The big rewrite. Small defaults get adopted. Big rewrites get
  debated. Keep the improvement small.
- Improvement that needs you. If it dies when you stop pushing, it
  was never adopted. Say how it survives.

### Follow-up prep

- "How did you get the team to adopt it?" Example screens plus the
  checklist default. Adoption by ease, not by mandate.
- "What resistance did you meet?" Name one real objection and how
  the small scope answered it. Smallness disarms most resistance.
- "How do you decide when a process needs changing?" When a cost
  repeats: the third time the same argument happens, or the second
  new joiner hits the same wall.
