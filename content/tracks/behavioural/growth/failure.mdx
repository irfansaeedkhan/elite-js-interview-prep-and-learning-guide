<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you failed" is different from the production
mistake question. A mistake is an incident. A failure is a judgement
call that turned out wrong, with consequences you cannot undo by
rolling back.

Interviewers score three things. Is the failure real, with a cost.
Do you own your part before explaining the context. And did the
lesson change a habit, not just a belief.

The trap is the small failure. "I failed a test once" or "I missed a
deadline by a day" dodges the question. Panels respect a real failure
told honestly far more than a safe one told smoothly.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "I led a migration to a new form library for our
checkout. I chose it mostly because I liked its API and its docs
looked clean."

**Task.** "My job was to pick a library that handled our checkout
edge cases, especially conditional validation across steps. I did not
test those cases before committing the team to the migration."

**Action.** "Two weeks in, we found the library could not do
conditional validation the way our checkout needed. I told the team
plainly that I had picked it on enthusiasm rather than evidence, and
I led the rollback myself. Then I wrote a short checklist for
library choices that starts with our edge cases and requires a
prototype against the hardest case before adoption."

**Result.** "The rollback cost us about two weeks. The checklist
stayed, and the next migration used it and shipped without a
rollback. What changed for me is small and specific: I no longer let
myself recommend a tool I have not tested against our hardest case."

The answer works because the failure is a judgement error, not bad
luck, and the lesson is a checklist, not a moral. The cost is stated
(two weeks), which is what makes the honesty believable.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "I decided [judgement call], based on [reason that
  turned out weak]." Name the weak reason honestly.
- **Task.** "The decision needed [the evidence you skipped]." Say
  what you skipped. That is the failure's mechanism.
- **Action.** "When it failed, I [owned it plainly, led the
  reversal, built the guardrail]." Ownership first, guardrail second.
  The guardrail is a checklist, a prototype rule, or a review step.
- **Result.** "[Cost stated plainly], and [the next time the
  guardrail worked]." A later success with the guardrail proves the
  lesson stuck.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I once failed to finish a feature on time because the
requirements kept changing. It was frustrating, but I learned that
communication is important and I try to communicate better now."

Why it fails: the failure is blamed on changing requirements, the
lesson is a moral ("communication is important"), and nothing changed
visibly. There is no judgement error and no habit.

Strong answer: "I led a migration to a new form library that I chose
mostly because I liked its API, and I did not check how it handled
our edge cases. Two weeks in we found it could not do conditional
validation the way our checkout needed, and we had to roll back. I
told the team I had picked it on enthusiasm rather than evidence,
and I wrote a short checklist for library choices that starts with
our edge cases."

Why it works: the error is a judgement call, owned in one sentence,
with a concrete cost and a concrete guardrail. The next migration
using the checklist is the proof.

### Traps

- Blaming context. Requirements, timelines, and teammates are
  context, not the failure. Your decision is the failure.
- The moral lesson. "I learned humility" is not a lesson. A checklist
  is.
- The trivial failure. If nothing cost anything, it is not the story
  for this question.
- Reusing your production mistake story. The panel asks both
  questions sometimes. Keep two different stories ready.

### Follow-up prep

- "What did that failure teach you about decisions?" Answer with the
  guardrail, not a philosophy. "Prototype against the hardest case
  first" is a decision rule.
- "How do you handle failure now compared to then?" Show the
  before and after as habits: enthusiasm first then, evidence first
  now.
- "Would you make the same choice again?" No, with the reason in one
  sentence. Do not relitigate the whole story.
