<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly" tests how
you learn, not what you know. Every job requires learning something
new under time pressure. Panels want the method.

Interviewers score three things. Did you have a plan, or did you
just read randomly. Did you turn learning into doing early, with
something small shipped. And did you leave something behind for the
next person.

The trap is the tutorial story. "I watched videos and read docs and
then I knew it" describes consumption, not learning. Learning shows
in a question asked, a thing built, or a note written.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Our team adopted a new state library, and I had two
weeks before I owned a feature built on it. I had never used it, and
the feature was customer facing."

**Task.** "I needed working knowledge fast, not expertise. The bar
was shipping the feature correctly, not knowing every feature of the
library."

**Action.** "I read the docs end to end first, which took a day,
then I rebuilt one small existing screen with the library instead of
starting on the feature. That gave me a safe place to get things
wrong. I wrote down every question the docs did not answer, and I
took that list to the teammate who had proposed the library. His
answers filled the gaps in one session, which beat days of guessing."

**Result.** "I shipped the feature on time, and my notes became the
onboarding doc for the next person who joined. The method stuck: docs
first, small rebuild second, questions list third, and I still learn
new tools that way."

The answer works because the method has four named steps and each
step produces something (a rebuilt screen, a question list, a doc).
That is learning made visible.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "I had [time] to learn [thing] before [real work
  that depended on it]." The deadline and the dependency create the
  pressure.
- **Task.** "The bar was [working knowledge for a specific job], not
  expertise." Saying the bar shows judgement about scope.
- **Action.** "I [docs first, small rebuild second, question list
  third, expert session fourth]." Name your steps in order. A method
  with steps beats "I studied hard."
- **Result.** "[Shipped work on time], and [what you left behind:
  notes, a doc, a guide]." Leaving something behind proves the
  learning was real and reusable.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I had to learn a new state library quickly, so I watched
a lot of tutorials and read the documentation, and after a couple of
weeks I felt comfortable with it and built the feature."

Why it fails: no method, no doing, no evidence. Watching and reading
are inputs, not learning, and "felt comfortable" is not a result.

Strong answer: "When our team adopted a new state library, I had two
weeks before I owned a feature built on it. I read the docs end to
end first, then rebuilt one small existing screen with it, and wrote
down every question the docs did not answer. I took that list to the
teammate who had proposed the library, and his answers filled the
gaps."

Why it works: four steps, each producing an artefact, and the expert
session is targeted by the question list instead of a vague "I asked
for help." The shipped feature and the onboarding doc close it.

### Traps

- Consumption as learning. Watching and reading alone are not a
  method. Build something small.
- No deadline pressure. Without a deadline, the story is just "I
  learned a thing." The question needs urgency.
- Solo learning only. Asking an expert with a prepared question list
  is a skill, not a weakness. Show it.
- No artefact left behind. Notes, a doc, or a guide prove the
  learning outlived the task.

### Follow-up prep

- "How do you approach learning a new technology?" Give your method
  in order, the same one from your story. Consistency is the point.
- "What do you do when documentation is poor?" Small rebuilds,
  source reading, and targeted questions to someone experienced.
- "How do you know when you know enough to start building?" When
  you can build the smallest real thing and explain what it does.
  That is the bar from your story.
