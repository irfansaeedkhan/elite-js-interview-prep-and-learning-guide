<TopicDefinition>

"Tell me about a time you received difficult feedback" tests whether
you can hear something unpleasant and use it. Everyone claims to
welcome feedback. The story is the proof.

Interviewers score three things. Did you listen instead of defending.
Did you understand the feedback well enough to repeat it. And did
something visible change afterwards.

The trap is the feedback you secretly disagree with. "My manager said
I was too quiet, but really I was just focused" is not a feedback
story. It is a defence. Pick feedback that was at least partly right,
and say so.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "In a review my lead told me my pull requests were
hard to review because I bundled refactoring with features. My first
reaction was defensive, because the refactoring felt necessary to me."

**Task.** "I needed to decide whether the feedback was right, and
not answer from the defensive feeling. So I gave it a day instead of
replying in the review."

**Action.** "The next day I asked him to show me one example. He
opened a recent pull request, and he was right: the diff mixed a
rename, a behaviour change, and the feature. I started splitting
refactoring into its own pull request before the feature, and I
started naming the split in the pull request description so reviewers
could see it."

**Result.** "My reviews got faster, and my last three features
merged without a single back and forth on scope. A month later I
told him the change had worked, without him asking, which closed the
loop."

The answer works because the defensive reaction is admitted, not
hidden, and the change is one habit with a measurable result. The
unprompted report-back is the detail that makes the growth believable.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "[Person] told me [feedback, quoted fairly]." Quote
  it fairly, even if it stung. Softening it signals you did not hear
  it.
- **Task.** "My first reaction was [honest reaction], so I [gave it
  time / asked for an example] before responding." The pause is a
  skill, not a delay.
- **Action.** "I changed [one visible habit]." One habit, not a
  personality overhaul. Name it precisely.
- **Result.** "[Measurable or observed change], and I [reported
  back unprompted]." Reporting back closes the loop and is rare
  enough to be memorable.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "My manager once said I needed to communicate more, but
I think I was actually communicating fine, I just worked differently.
I tried to speak up more in meetings after that."

Why it fails: the speaker disagrees with the feedback, so nothing was
really heard. The change is vague ("speak up more") and the result is
missing entirely.

Strong answer: "In a review my lead told me my pull requests were
hard to review because I bundled refactoring with features. My first
reaction was defensive, because the refactoring felt necessary to me.
I sat on it for a day, then asked him to show me one example, and he
was right, the diff mixed two purposes. Now I split refactoring into
its own pull request before the feature."

Why it works: the reaction is honest, the verification is concrete
(one example), the change is one habit, and the result is stated in
the next sentence of the full answer. It shows hearing, not just
agreeing.

### Traps

- Feedback you disagree with. If you did not accept it, pick another
  story.
- The vague change. "I worked on communication" is not a change. A
  habit with a name is.
- No result. Without a result, the panel cannot tell whether the
  feedback mattered.
- The feedback sandwich about yourself. Do not praise yourself for
  handling feedback well. Let the changed habit do it.

### Follow-up prep

- "How do you usually react to criticism?" Name the pause honestly.
  "I sit on it a day, then I ask for an example" is a real answer.
- "Have you ever disagreed with feedback you received?" Yes is
  fine, if the disagreement was handled with evidence and respect.
  The disagreement topic is good preparation.
- "What feedback are you working on right now?" Have a current one
  ready. It proves the habit is alive, not a single story.
