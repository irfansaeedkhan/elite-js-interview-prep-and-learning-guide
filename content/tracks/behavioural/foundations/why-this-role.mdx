<TopicDefinition>

"Why do you want this role?" tests whether you researched this team
or just applied everywhere. Interviewers can tell the difference in
one sentence.

They score two things. Do you know what the team actually works on.
And does your past give you a reason to want that work specifically,
rather than any frontend job.

The trap is flattery plus perks. "Great culture, exciting product,
room to grow" fits every company, so it proves nothing. If your answer
works for ten other job posts, it fails this one.

</TopicDefinition>

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Your team is rebuilding the checkout experience, and
the job post mentions accessibility as a requirement, not a nice
extra."

**Task.** "I wanted a role where the work I am best at is the work
the team actually needs, so I looked for a team rebuilding the exact
surface I have spent a year on."

**Action.** "In my current role I rebuilt checkout validation and
added a review step before payment. I also started fixing keyboard
and screen reader issues on our forms, but that work is still a side
project there, not part of the definition of done."

**Result.** "Checkout completion rose from 61 percent to 74 percent.
I want to bring that experience to a team where accessibility is
already part of how done is defined, and keep growing the
accessibility part here."

The answer works because every claim points at this team. Swap the
team, and the answer breaks. That is the test.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "Your team is working on [specific surface or
  problem from the job post or product]." One sentence of research.
- **Task.** "I am looking for [the same kind of work], because
  [one line on why it matters to you]."
- **Action.** "In my current role I [proof from your past that
  matches the team's problem]." Numbers beat adjectives.
- **Result.** "I can bring [the proof] and I want to grow [the gap
  this team fills]." End on contribution, not perks.

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "I have heard great things about your culture, and I am
excited by the product, and I want to grow as a developer. This feels
like a great next step for my career."

Why it fails: nothing in it is specific to this team. It could be
said to any company, so it signals no research and no real reason.

Strong answer: "I want this role because your team is rebuilding the
checkout experience, and that is the work I have done best. In my
current role I rebuilt form validation and a review step, and
checkout completion rose from 61 percent to 74 percent. I also want
to work somewhere accessibility is part of the definition of done,
because your job post lists it as a requirement rather than a nice
extra."

Why it works: it names the team's work, backs the claim with a
result, and points at a specific line in the job post. Research is
visible in every sentence.

### Traps

- Praising the brand. Praise the work, not the logo.
- Listing perks (remote work, salary, stack). Perks explain why you
  applied, not why you fit.
- Claiming passion for a product you have never used. Try the
  product before the interview, or be honest about what you did
  research.
- A five minute company history. One sentence of research is enough.

### Follow-up prep

- "What do you know about our product?" Use it before the interview.
  One real observation beats a feature list.
- "Where do you see yourself in two years?" Name the work you want
  to be doing, at a level this team can offer. Keep it consistent
  with your answer here.
- "What other roles are you considering?" Be honest but brief. "I
  am talking to a few product teams, and this one stands out because
  of the checkout work" keeps the focus on this role.
