<TopicDefinition>

"What is your greatest weakness?" tests self-awareness, not honesty
as a confession. Interviewers want to see that you know a real gap,
that it is not fatal for this role, and that you are already fixing
it.

They score three things. Is the weakness real. Is it bounded, meaning
it does not disqualify you for this job. And is there a fix with
evidence, not just a promise.

The trap has two forms. The fake weakness ("I am a perfectionist")
insults the interviewer. The fatal weakness ("I struggle to finish
work on time" for a delivery role) disqualifies you. Pick the middle:
real, not core to the role, already improving.

<TopicTab value="examples">

Here is what a strong answer sounds like. This is an illustrative
example, not your story. Swap in your own facts using the skeleton
below.

**Situation.** "Early last year I noticed I was approving code
reviews I had doubts about. I told myself I was being kind and fast,
but really I was avoiding friction."

**Task.** "I needed to give honest feedback without becoming the
person who blocks every pull request."

**Action.** "I changed two habits. First, I comment on behaviour,
not people, so I write 'this validation message confused me as a
reader' instead of 'this is unclear.' Second, I ask one question
before approving, which forces me to read the change properly instead
of skimming it."

**Result.** "A vague validation message I once would have approved
shipped before I changed this, and support got the complaints. Since
the change I have not approved doubtful work, reviews take me a bit
longer, and two teammates have said the comments help them catch
issues earlier."

The answer works because the weakness is real, the fix is concrete,
and the evidence is someone else's words, not a self-rating.

</TopicTab>

<TopicTab value="indepth">

### STAR skeleton

- **Situation.** "I used to [real weakness, stated plainly]." One
  sentence. No softening, no joke.
- **Task.** "That caused [one concrete cost]." The cost proves the
  weakness is real and that you noticed it yourself.
- **Action.** "I changed [specific habit or system]." Name the
  mechanism, not the intention. "I try harder" is not a mechanism.
- **Result.** "Since then [evidence the fix works]." Evidence from
  someone else, or a number, beats "I think I improved."

### Strong vs weak

Weak answer: "My greatest weakness is that I am a perfectionist. I
care too much about quality and sometimes I spend too long polishing
details."

Why it fails: it is a strength in disguise, every interviewer has
heard it, and it shows no self-awareness at all. It also has no fix,
because a fake weakness needs none.

Strong answer: "I used to avoid giving critical feedback in code
reviews. I would approve work I had doubts about because I did not
want to slow someone down. That caused real problems once, when a
vague validation message I had doubts about shipped and support got
the complaints. Now I leave specific, behaviour-focused comments and
I ask one question before approving any review."

Why it works: the weakness is real and specific, it is not core to
writing code, and the fix is a habit with evidence. The story admits
a cost, which is what makes it believable.

### Traps

- The disguised strength. If your weakness sounds like a compliment,
  pick another one.
- The fatal weakness. Never name a gap in the core skill of the role
  you want.
- The fixed weakness with no evidence. "I fixed it" needs a result or
  someone else's observation.
- Keeping talking after the result. End the answer. Silence after a
  good answer is fine; rambling after it is not.

### Follow-up prep

- "What feedback have you received recently?" Have one real piece
  ready. It should rhyme with your weakness answer, not contradict
  it.
- "How do you handle criticism?" Show one change you made because of
  criticism. The change is the proof.
- "Tell me about a time you failed." That is a separate question in
  this track. Keep the two stories different, or the panel will
  notice you have only one story.
